1. The memory wall

Modern GPUs can execute trillions of operations per second, but they are increasingly starved for data. The industry's answer is HBM, which stacks several DRAM dies vertically and connects them to the processor through a silicon interposer. Because the interposer is manufactured by TSMC using its CoWoS packaging process, supply of HBM-equipped accelerators is constrained less by logic wafers than by advanced packaging capacity.

This matters for inference workloads in particular. When a large language model generates tokens autoregressively, each step reads the full KV cache from memory, so throughput is bounded by bandwidth rather than by FLOPS. Vendors therefore quote memory bandwidth in TB/s alongside peak compute, and hyperscalers increasingly evaluate chips on tokens-per-second-per-dollar rather than on raw TFLOPS.

2. Power, cooling and the ESCO opportunity

A single rack of accelerators can now draw over 100 kW, which exceeds what traditional air cooling can remove. Operators are moving to direct-to-chip liquid cooling and, in some cases, full immersion. Utilities and ESCOs have responded with Energy-as-a-Service contracts, under which the provider finances the microgrid, the UPS systems and the chillers, and the data-center tenant pays a fee indexed to uptime and PUE.

For investors, these contracts resemble infrastructure assets: long-dated, inflation-linked cash flows with downside protection from take-or-pay clauses. Several European ESCOs trading at single-digit EV/EBITDA multiples have consequently attracted interest from private-equity sponsors pursuing a Buy-and-Build strategy, where a platform company acquires regional installers to capture procurement synergies and multiple expansion.

3. Export controls and the geopolitics of compute

Since 2022, BIS has repeatedly tightened export controls on advanced semiconductors and SME destined for China. The rules use thresholds such as TPP and performance density to define which chips require a license, and they extend to foreign-produced items under the FDPR. Chinese firms have responded by accelerating domestic alternatives, while hyperscalers in the Gulf have negotiated bilateral arrangements to import accelerators under end-use monitoring.

Analysts at think tanks such as CSIS and PIIE argue that the controls have raised the cost of frontier training in China without halting it, and that the second-order effect has been a boom in demand for inference-optimised ASICs and for refurbished A100-class hardware traded through intermediaries in Southeast Asia.

4. What to watch

Three indicators will shape the next eighteen months: CoWoS capacity additions announced by TSMC, the pricing of HBM4 relative to HBM3E, and whether sovereign AI programmes in Europe and the Middle East translate into firm capex rather than MOUs. For LBO underwriting in adjacent sectors, the key sensitivity remains the utilisation assumption embedded in EaaS contracts, since a 10-point drop in utilisation can erase the equity IRR entirely.
